01What this role owns

Mindvalley is a 22-year-old personal growth education company. It sells transformation — Quests, Membership, Masteries, Certifications, Summits — to a global consumer audience, with Vishen Lakhiani as the founder and primary voice.

Email is not a support function here. It is a primary revenue channel.

One always-on lifecycle journey has produced seven figures in attributed revenue. A single summit announcement reaches millions of sends across segments.

You own

  • The email calendar — what goes out, to whom, when, in what language. Nothing ships that isn't on it.
  • Copy quality and brand voice — the last line between a draft and five million inboxes.
  • The governance process — you are the mandatory reviewer.
  • The daily broadcast slot — the everyday send to the broadcast list.
  • Campaign sequences — Invite, Show-up and Sales for every launch.
  • Lifecycle journeys — onboarding, reactivation, upsell, trial, churn.
  • Performance reporting — the weekly read, the MOW dashboard, campaign retros.

You don't own

Vishen's weekly newsletter — Ramya retains this. Also: transactional email (receipts, confirmations), cohort-start logistics, customer support comms, ad creative and paid media, the product roadmap.

The newsletter carve-out

Ramya continues to own Vishen's weekly newsletter after the handover — the sync, the draft, the distribution, the translation handoff. It is the one email that does not route through the Email Lead. Three things follow, and all three need saying out loud rather than discovering later.

  1. Review authority has one exception. You are the mandatory reviewer for every email at Mindvalley except the newsletter, which Ramya owns and approves. Anyone unsure which applies should ask, not guess.
  2. The calendar still has one owner: you. The newsletter takes a slot like anything else. When a campaign send and the newsletter collide on a day, a segment or a suppression list, you flag it and Ramya decides. This happens more than you'd expect — a recent summit announcement and that week's newsletter ended up merged into one very large combined send.
  3. Frequency capping is a shared problem. The newsletter reaches nearly every segment weekly, and your campaign volume sits on top of it. Watch total weekly touches per segment, not just your own.

The person behind the list

Behind every segment is one person. Internally we call her Julie.

  • 38–52. Solopreneur, freelancer, consultant, coach or small-team founder.
  • Knows the topic matters. Feels behind. Working late. Kids growing up.
  • Has bought courses she didn't finish, and carries guilt about it.
  • Brilliant and exhausted. Not technical.
  • Not exclusively an entrepreneur — most of the list are non-founders. A listicle aimed at "10 founders" lost its test for exactly this reason.

The 7am test. Julie is making coffee. Her teenager just left for school. She has 47 emails. She sees yours.

Would she open it? Read every word? Feel something? Click?

If any sentence breaks that flow — cut it.

03Mindvalley tone of voice

Mindvalley emails are one human writing to one human. Usually that human is Vishen. The voice is raw and personal, story-first, outcome-led, generous, and uncynical — we genuinely believe people can change, so we never sell with fear.

This is the register to aim for, from the Expert to Authority sequence:

I was sitting at Soho House in Stockholm. I came to Stockholm to attend a longevity conference. I assembled people around me: founders, a few Mindvalley authors, close friends. I asked them an important question. I'd gathered them because I was confused. And I wanted the truth from people who wouldn't flatter me.

Specific place. Specific discomfort. No adjectives doing the work.

The six principles every email is built on

PrincipleSourceIn practice

The four emotional stages

Every email in a sequence sits in exactly one stage:

  1. Seen (emails 1–2) — "Someone understands my life." No solutions yet. Just the mirror.
  2. Hope (3–5) — "Maybe this time is different." Tiny wins. A student story. A 90-second demo.
  3. Trust (6–8) — "These people know what they're doing." Method reveal, credibility, objection killing.
  4. Committed (9–12) — Stop selling. Cement. Help her clear her calendar.

The locked rules

Never say

  • "Productivity" → say time freedom or free your Friday
  • Fear of job loss, or AI replacing humans
  • Jargon: MCP, n8n, agentic workflows, vibe coding, RAG
  • "Master prompting" — creates false expectations
  • "Entrepreneurs" or "founders" exclusively
  • "Pitch" → always invitation
  • "We're excited to announce…"
  • "Don't miss this incredible opportunity!"
  • More than one CTA in a single email

Always

  • "For the first time" for genuine first-ever reveals
  • Lead with outcomes, not mechanisms
  • "No replays. No recordings." from email 7 on live events
  • One CTA per email
  • At least one specific name, number or sensory detail
  • 250–350 words for pre-event emails
  • Under 1,000 characters for WhatsApp

Anatomy of a single email

  1. Subject line — one job: get opened. Would you text this to a friend? If no, rewrite.
  2. First sentence — short, unexpected, personal. A scene, a question, a confession.
    ✓ "I almost didn't send this email."   ✓ "Last Tuesday at 11pm, my phone buzzed."   ✗ "I'm excited to announce…"
  3. Story or scene — 2–4 short paragraphs. Mirror, then shift.
  4. The bridge — one sentence that reframes. An insight, not a lesson.
  5. CTA — one action, framed as service: "If this resonated, I made something for you."

04How to come up with emails

Ideas are not invented at the keyboard. They are harvested from five standing sources.

  1. Message of the Week — the Monday 16:30 meeting sets the company's single message for the week, and every channel aligns behind it. Your week's emails start here.
  2. Vishen directly — the weekly Newsletter Sync and Strategy Deep Dive. His raw stories are the highest-performing asset we have. Capture them verbatim; don't smooth them out.
  3. The campaign calendar — every launch has a fixed sequence architecture. You're filling known slots, not inventing structure.
  4. Performance data — last week's winners tell you what angle to run again.
  5. The audience's own words — summit transcripts, onboarding surveys, support tickets, NPS verbatims. Full of Julie's exact phrasing. Steal it.

The biggest lever we have found

When Vishen gives people something to do, they become active participants rather than passive recipients. Put an action inside the email. Example — the Freedom Audit:

  1. Write down every task you did last week.
  2. Next to each, write H (human only), A (AI could do this), or B (between).
  3. Count your A's and B's. Estimate the hours. That's your Freedom Number.

The reader produces her own conversion argument. Never explain why it works. Just give it.

Getting one email live

  1. Create the Airtable record under Emails. All mandatory fields, or it won't be reviewed: Campaign Name · Email Type · Target Audience · Objective · Subject Line · Preview Text · Email Copy · CTA · Send Date & Time · Owner.
  2. Status → Ready for Review. This notifies the reviewer in Slack.
  3. Review — messaging clarity, campaign alignment, brand voice, conversion optimisation, CTA clarity, segmentation relevance. Outcome: Approved, or Changes Required.
  4. Status → Approved for Execution. Only now can the email be built, assets finalised, scheduling begun.
  5. Build in Braze. QA links, personalization tokens, segmentation, tracking (UTMs and both otags), formatting on mobile and desktop.
  6. Status → Scheduled, then Live once sent.
  7. Log it in the master tracker — one row per segment.

Suppression discipline. Always suppress people who already converted. A live summit WhatsApp DM, for instance, targeted only non-members and members who had not clicked the invite emails or that week's newsletter, with all summit sign-ups suppressed. Sending a registration invite to someone who already registered is the fastest way to burn trust and inflate unsubscribes.

06Governance — the non-negotiables

No email goes live without review status approved.

If it is not in Airtable → it does not exist.
If it is not reviewed → it does not go live.

Draft→Ready for Review→Approved→In Execution→Scheduled→Live

No skipping stages. No direct Draft → Live.

The five rules

  1. Airtable is the source of truth. No Slack approvals. No WhatsApp approvals. No verbal approvals.
  2. Review is mandatory. If it's not marked Approved, it is not approved.
  3. Late changes. Inside 2 hours of send, the requester updates directly in the ESP — or the email is postponed.
  4. Ownership clarity. Every email has exactly one Owner (accountable) and one Reviewer (approver).
  5. No shadow emails. Nothing built outside Airtable, sent without a record, or executed ad hoc.

Scope: all promotional, campaign, lifecycle, automated and broadcast email — anything influencing revenue or engagement. Exempt: pure transactional, cohort logistics, support comms. If unclear, include it.

Enforcement: emails sent outside this process get flagged, reviewed retroactively, and can cost the sender their execution access.

Zin — the hardest part of this job is not writing. It is holding this line when a senior stakeholder wants to skip it. The SOP is what makes that a process decision rather than a personal one. Use it.

  • One row per segment, not per email. A newsletter to nine segments is nine rows.

  • Log sends before they go out, from the calendar. Backfill results after.
  • Campaign rollups live in per-campaign sheets, e.g. "Expert to Authority Summit Sept 2026 — leads breakdown".

Reading — start with the right benchmark

Each sequence type has its own target band for open rate, CTR, CTOR and a maximum unsubscribe rate. The current bands — and where we actually land against them — are maintained on the Benchmarks tab of the master tracker. Check there rather than from memory, because they move.

Show-up sequences run far ahead of invite sequences on every metric, because the audience has already said yes. Never compare across sequence types. Hitting the band means you met standard expectations — it does not mean you won.

Obsess over CTOR. Open rate is largely the subject line and the list temperature. CTOR is your copy, isolated from both.

Reading — always ask "compared to what?"

Structural differences distort everything. A worked example: one summit's Email 1 looked far bigger than a comparable summit's — but only because it was a combined send, pairing the announcement with that week's newsletter across many more segments, including the translated ones. The first won on reach; the second won on engagement rate. Both are true — say both.

Then diagnose rather than describe. A low open rate on a cold list is expected for awareness. A mediocre one on a broad unsegmented list means segment further. A soft number on a just-announced summit means the remind sequence hasn't run yet. Name the cause, not just the number.
