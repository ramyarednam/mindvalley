Podcast Naming Case

Vishen Lakhiani podcast suggestions

We're choosing the name based on four things: trigger an emotion, new audience expansion, fit with current audience, and an actionable podcast.

01 — The case

How we're choosing the name

Research on podcast naming is consistent: the names that break out don't describe the topic — they name a feeling the listener wants, stay short enough to say out loud, and leave room to grow. The four things below turn that into our checklist.

01

Emotion

The name has to make someone feel something before they press play. Feeling is what turns a listener into a fan, and a fan into word of mouth.

02

Audience expansion

Our core is around 45. The name has to pull the audience down to 30-plus — the new wave of solopreneurs, founders and creators — without losing the base.

03

Fit with Mindvalley & Vishen

It has to sound like aspiration — the thing Mindvalley and Vishen have always stood for. That fit is what makes the reach believable.

04

Actionable

This is a free resource that builds trust by being usable. Every episode ends with a real tool — so the name has to promise action, not just talk.

The name creates the aspiration. The tool makes it real.

Here's why this matters now

Our average listener age is around 45 — a strong, loyal base, but the growth is below it.

AI has created an enormous new audience that barely existed five years ago: solopreneurs, digital nomads, first-time founders, AI creators.

Everyone is a creator now, and all of them are hunting the same thing — free, actionable learning. This podcast is that free resource, and it's our doorway to build trust with a new audience.

So the strategy is simple: keep the aspirational core that earned Vishen a mature, established audience of over 10M, and use this new podcast to pull in the younger, faster, action-hungry 30-plus age wave. That's how you own a bigger audience instead of a fixed one — and the name is the front door.

References we studied: The Game (Alex Hormozi), The Diary of a CEO (Steven Bartlett), Modern Wisdom (Chris Williamson), On Purpose (Jay Shetty), The Mel Robbins Podcast.

What the research says a strong name needs

Across every credible naming source, the same short list recurs — and all three picks clear it:

Rule 1

Short & sayable

2–4 words that survive a thumbnail and roll off the tongue. Word of mouth is the growth engine.

Rule 2

Name the feeling, not the topic

Aspirational beats descriptive. Point at the transformation, and leave room to grow past one subject.

Rule 3

Own your name in search

In 2026, ranking on your own name matters more than ever — the host lockup makes us unmistakable to search and to AI summaries.

Rule 4

Don't box yourself in

A name the show can outgrow into. "The Bigger Life" and "The Human Side" both stretch across every pillar.

03 — The process

The process we run every week

The name sets the promise; the process keeps it. This is the operating model — the same spine whichever name wins — built so the emotion the name creates gets delivered in every episode.

Step 1 · Prep

A 5,000-word brief

Every episode starts with a deep, guest-specific research brief — the same standard the top shows run on. It's where the conversation and the tool hand-off get designed before we ever record.

Step 2 · Actionable

Pair each episode with a real tool

The differentiator: every episode sends the listener away with something they can use — a Blink Learn session, a Vibrantly stack, a personalised plan. The tool is decided alongside the guest, so the value is built in, not bolted on.

Step 3 · Fact-check & QA

Deeper research, notes & clear terms

Every claim is checked before publish, with on-screen notes and plain-language explanations of any complex terms — so the audience can trust the advice and go research further themselves.

Step 4 · Distribution

One date, every channel

Align a calendar date and drop the episode on YouTube, Spotify, Apple, Substack and social channels the same day. Start at a repeatable cadence — 2 per month, moving to 1 per week once it gains traction.

The bigger picture, à la Steven Bartlett: a research machine feeding a fixed cadence, one owner per stage, same-day distribution everywhere. That's how a show becomes a habit.
